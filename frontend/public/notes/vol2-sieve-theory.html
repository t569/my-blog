The method that almost works

Sieve theory is the study of a technique together with an exact description of why it cannot finish the job. No other area of mathematics knows its own limits this precisely — the parity problem is a theorem about what a whole family of proofs can never do.

§ 0The one-sentence version

A sieve replaces the question "is this number prime?" — which is about multiplicative structure and is hard — with the question "how many members of my set survive removing all multiples of small primes?" — which is about counting and is easy. The entire theory is the study of the gap between those two questions.

That trade is fabulously profitable and provably insufficient. Profitable: Brun proved in 1919 that the twin primes have a convergent reciprocal sum, using nothing but clever bookkeeping with inclusion–exclusion. Insufficient: no argument of this shape will ever prove there are infinitely many twin primes, and we can say precisely why.

Start with the object everyone knows. To find the primes up to 100, write out 2, 3, …, 100, then cross out every multiple of 2 (except 2), every multiple of 3 (except 3), every multiple of 5, every multiple of 7. Stop — \(11^2 > 100\), so anything still standing is prime. That's the sieve of Eratosthenes, 2200 years old.

Sieve theory asks a quantitative version: don't list the survivors, count them, and get an answer accurate enough to be useful. Crossing out multiples of 2 removes about half. Of the rest, multiples of 3 remove about a third. And so on — the estimate \(x\prod_{p\le z}(1-1/p)\) writes itself.

The trouble is the word "about". Every "about" is an error, and there are exponentially many of them. Making that error smaller than the thing you're estimating is the entire subject.

Notation, fixed once
  • \(\mathcal{A}\) — a finite set (or sequence) of integers. For twin primes, \(\mathcal{A} = \{n(n+2) : n \le x\}\).
  • \(\mathcal{P}\) — a set of primes to sift by; \(P(z) = \prod_{p \in \mathcal{P},\, p < z} p\).
  • \(\mathcal{A}_d = \{a \in \mathcal{A} : d \mid a\}\), and \(|\mathcal{A}_d| = X g(d) + r_d\) — the sieve axiom: a smooth main term \(Xg(d)\) with \(g\) multiplicative, plus a remainder \(r_d\) you hope is small.
  • \(S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) = \#\{a \in \mathcal{A} : \gcd(a, P(z)) = 1\}\) — the sifting function, the thing we want.

Everything a sieve is allowed to know is the list of numbers \(|\mathcal{A}_d|\). Hold on to that — §4 turns it into an impossibility theorem.

§ 1Legendre's sieve, and why it fails

Inclusion–exclusion is exact:

\[ S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) = \sum_{d \mid P(z)} \mu(d)\,|\mathcal{A}_d| = X\sum_{d\mid P(z)}\mu(d)g(d) \;+\; \sum_{d \mid P(z)}\mu(d)\,r_d. \]

For \(\mathcal{A} = \{n \le x\}\) and \(g(d) = 1/d\), the main term is the familiar \(x\prod_{p<z}(1-1/p)\), and by Mertens that's \(\sim x e^{-\gamma}/\log z\).

1.1The error term eats the main term

Take \(r_d = |\mathcal{A}_d| - x/d = -\{x/d\}\), so \(|r_d| < 1\). Innocuous, individually. But the sum has one term for every squarefree divisor of \(P(z)\):

\[ \left|\sum_{d\mid P(z)}\mu(d)r_d\right| \le 2^{\pi(z)}. \]

Meanwhile the main term is only about \(x e^{-\gamma}/\log z\). So the error dominates as soon as \(2^{\pi(z)} \gtrsim x\), i.e. as soon as \(\pi(z) \gtrsim \log_2 x\), i.e. \(z \gtrsim \log x\log\log x\). Legendre's sieve is exact and completely useless: it can only sift by primes up to about \(\log x\), and survivors of that are not primes, they're just numbers with no small factors.

The formula is correct. That is not the problem.

Inclusion–exclusion is the rule you already know. To count the people in a room who are neither wearing hats nor glasses: take everyone, subtract the hat-wearers, subtract the glasses-wearers — but now you have subtracted the people wearing both twice, so add them back. With three properties you alternate again, and so on.

Applied to sieving, that is exactly Legendre's formula, and it is an identity: no approximation anywhere. So why is this section titled "why it fails"?

Because of how the errors are counted, not how big they are

The formula has one term for every combination of sifting primes you can form. If you are sifting by the primes below \(z\), there are \(\pi(z)\) of them, and the number of combinations is \(2^{\pi(z)}\) — one for every subset.

Each term needs the count \(|\mathcal{A}_d|\), which you only know approximately: \(|\mathcal{A}_d| = x/d + r_d\) where \(|r_d| < 1\). An error of less than 1 is as good as an error can get. But you have \(2^{\pi(z)}\) of them, and they need not cancel.

Imagine measuring a room by adding up a million rulers, each accurate to a millimetre. Every individual measurement is excellent. The total could be off by a kilometre. The number of error terms matters more than the size of each one — and this is the single most important habit of mind in the subject.
Doing the arithmetic on that

The quantity you are estimating is roughly \(x/\log z\). The error budget is \(2^{\pi(z)}\). The method is useful only while \(2^{\pi(z)}\) stays below \(x\) — that is, while \(\pi(z) \lesssim \log_2 x\), which means \(z\) can be no bigger than about \(\log x\).

Now compare that with what you want. To catch primes up to \(x\), you must sift by every prime up to \(\sqrt x\). So you need \(z = \sqrt x\) and you can afford \(z = \log x\).

Drag \(x\) in the figure below from \(10^3\) to \(10^{30}\) — a factor of \(10^{27}\) — and watch the usable \(z\) creep from about 20 to about 510, while \(\sqrt x\) climbs to \(10^{15}\). That is not a gap you close by being more careful. It needs a different idea, and §2 is that idea.

§ 2Brun: stop insisting on the truth

Brun's idea, in one line: an identity has exponentially many terms; an inequality does not need them all.

Bonferroni inequalities

For every \(m \ge 0\), \[ \sum_{\substack{d \mid P(z)\\ \omega(d)\le m}}\mu(d)|\mathcal{A}_d| \;\ge\; S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) \quad (m \text{ even}), \qquad \le \; S \quad (m \text{ odd}). \]

Truncating at \(\omega(d)\le m\) leaves only \(\sum_{j\le m}\binom{\pi(z)}{j} \le \pi(z)^m\) terms — polynomial in \(\pi(z)\) rather than exponential. Choosing \(m \asymp \log\log z\) makes the truncation loss a factor \(1+o(1)\) while keeping the error term under control. That single manoeuvre opened the subject.

Why does chopping the sum give a bound rather than nonsense? Fix one element \(a\) with exactly \(\nu\) of the sifting primes dividing it. In the full sum \(\sum_{d\mid \gcd(a,P)}\mu(d)\), it contributes \(\sum_{j=0}^{\nu}\binom{\nu}{j}(-1)^j = (1-1)^\nu\), which is \(1\) if \(\nu = 0\) (i.e. \(a\) survives) and \(0\) otherwise. Perfect detection.

If you stop at \(j \le m\), that element contributes the partial alternating sum \(\sum_{j\le m}\binom{\nu}{j}(-1)^j = (-1)^m\binom{\nu-1}{m}\). That is \(\ge 0\) when \(m\) is even and \(\le 0\) when \(m\) is odd — a standard binomial fact. So every single element is counted with the right sign of error, and summing preserves the inequality. The bound is never wrong, only wasteful.

2.1Brun's theorem, and what it feels like

Brun's sieve gives \(\pi_2(x) \ll x(\log\log x)^2/(\log x)^2\), and hence

Brun, 1919

\(\displaystyle B = \sum_{p,\,p+2 \text{ both prime}}\left(\frac1p + \frac1{p+2}\right) < \infty.\)

Set against Euler's \(\sum_p 1/p = \infty\), this says the twin primes are a vanishingly thin subset of the primes — thin enough that their reciprocals converge. It is the first theorem ever proved about twin primes, and note what it is not: it does not say there are infinitely many. A convergent sum is compatible with a finite one.

§ 3Selberg: optimise instead of truncate

Brun throws away terms. Selberg (1947) does something cleverer: he keeps all of them but lets the coefficients be free variables, then minimises.

The trick is one line and is worth memorising. For any real numbers \(\lambda_d\) with \(\lambda_1 = 1\),

\[ \mathbf{1}[\gcd(a,P(z))=1] \;\le\; \Bigg(\sum_{\substack{d \mid \gcd(a,P(z))\\ d \le D}} \lambda_d\Bigg)^{\!2} \]

because the left side is 0 or 1, and when it is 1 the only surviving term on the right is \(d=1\), giving \(\lambda_1^2 = 1\). Squares are non-negative, so the inequality is free. Summing over \(a\):

\[ S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) \;\le\; \sum_{d_1,d_2 \le D}\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}\,|\mathcal{A}_{[d_1,d_2]}| \;\approx\; X\!\!\sum_{d_1,d_2\le D}\!\!\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}\,g([d_1,d_2]). \]

That is a quadratic form in the \(\lambda\), subject to one linear constraint. Minimising a positive-definite quadratic form under a linear constraint is undergraduate linear algebra; Selberg's contribution was finding the substitution that diagonalises it, giving the closed form

\[ \min_{\lambda_1=1} Q(\lambda) = \frac{1}{G(D)}, \qquad G(D) = \sum_{\substack{d \le D,\ d\mid P(z)\\ \mu^2(d)=1}}\ \prod_{p\mid d}\frac{g(p)}{1-g(p)}. \]

Why squaring anything gives you a free inequality

Start from something you cannot argue with: a real number squared is never negative. Selberg's whole sieve is that observation, aimed carefully.

Pick any numbers \(\lambda_d\) you like, with the single requirement \(\lambda_1 = 1\). Now look at

\[ \left(\sum_{d \mid \gcd(a, P(z)),\ d \le D} \lambda_d\right)^2. \]

Ask what this is worth for a particular \(a\). If \(a\) survived the sieve, then \(\gcd(a,P(z)) = 1\), so the only divisor in range is \(d=1\), and the sum is just \(\lambda_1 = 1\). Squared: exactly 1. If \(a\) did not survive, the sum is some other number — and its square is \(\ge 0\), whatever it happens to be.

So this expression is \(1\) on survivors and \(\ge 0\) on everything else. Summing it over all \(a\) therefore gives an upper bound for the number of survivors. The inequality cost nothing and required no cleverness — only the fact that squares are non-negative.

It is the same manoeuvre as bounding a count by \(\sum (\text{something})^2\) in a Cauchy–Schwarz argument: you deliberately overshoot in a way you can control, then spend your effort making the overshoot small.
And now it is an optimisation problem, not a guess

Brun had to choose where to truncate, and a bad choice wastes the bound. Selberg's version has no choice to make: expanding the square turns the right-hand side into a quadratic form in the \(\lambda\)'s — a sum of terms \(\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2} \cdot (\text{something})\) — subject to one linear constraint \(\lambda_1 = 1\).

Minimising a positive-definite quadratic form under a linear constraint is a standard exercise in linear algebra: there is a unique answer and you can solve for it. Selberg found the change of variables that diagonalises the form, producing the closed expression \(1/G(D)\).

The lesson the figure is built to deliver

Set \(D = 30\) in the panel below and press Try \(\lambda = \mu\). You land exactly on the theoretical optimum, and exactly on the true density. At full level, Selberg's sieve is inclusion–exclusion, and it loses nothing at all.

So every loss in a real application comes from one place: the restriction \(d \le D\). And that restriction exists only because the error terms \(r_d\) must stay controllable. You are never limited by the sieve. You are limited by your level of distribution — which is why Bombieri–Vinogradov and Elliott–Halberstam are the load-bearing theorems of the whole field.

Where the level restriction comes from

The neglected error is \(\sum_{d_1,d_2\le D}|\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}r_{[d_1,d_2]}| \le \max|\lambda|^2\sum_{d\le D^2}3^{\omega(d)}|r_d|\). To keep it below the main term \(X/G(D)\), you need control of \(r_d\) on average up to \(D^2\) — the level of distribution. For \(\mathcal{A}=\{n\le x\}\) that is free. For \(\mathcal{A}=\{p-2 : p\le x\}\) it is the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem, giving level \(x^{1/2-\varepsilon}\); pushing past \(1/2\) is the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture and is exactly what Zhang had to partially achieve. Level of distribution is the currency of the entire field.

§ 4The parity problem

Here is the wall. It is not a gap in our cleverness; it is a theorem about the information a sieve is given.

Recall the one constraint from §0: a sieve knows only the numbers \(|\mathcal{A}_d|\). Whatever bound it produces must be valid for every set with those same counts. So to prove a sieve cannot do something, exhibit two sets with identical counts and wildly different answers.

Selberg's construction does exactly that, using the Liouville function \(\lambda(n) = (-1)^{\Omega(n)}\), where \(\Omega\) counts prime factors with multiplicity.

The parity obstruction (Selberg)

Let \(\mathcal{A}^{+} = \{n \le x : \Omega(n) \text{ even}\}\) and \(\mathcal{A}^{-} = \{n\le x : \Omega(n)\text{ odd}\}\). For every \(d\), \[ |\mathcal{A}^{\pm}_d| = \frac{1}{2}\frac{x}{d} + O\!\left(\text{small}\right), \] because \(\sum_{n\le x,\ d\mid n}\lambda(n) = o(x/d)\). The two sets are therefore indistinguishable to any sieve. But every prime lies in \(\mathcal{A}^{-}\) (primes have \(\Omega = 1\)) and none in \(\mathcal{A}^{+}\).

Consequence: a sieve fed the axioms \(X = x/2,\ g(d)=1/d\) must return a lower bound that is valid for \(\mathcal{A}^{+}\) — whose sifted count is essentially zero — and so its lower bound is \(\le 0\). No sieve of this type can ever prove a sifted set is non-empty. And its upper bound must accommodate \(\mathcal{A}^{-}\), which is why sieve upper bounds are off by a factor of about 2 (or 4, in dimension 2) and never converge to the truth.

Why isn't this just a failure of imagination? Because of how the argument is structured. We are not saying "nobody has found a clever enough sieve". We are saying: fix any procedure whatsoever whose only inputs are the numbers \(|\mathcal{A}_d|\) and which always outputs a correct lower bound. Feed it \(\mathcal{A}^{+}\). Correctness forces the output to be \(\le 0\), because the truth for \(\mathcal{A}^{+}\) is \(0\). Now feed it \(\mathcal{A}^{-}\) — but the inputs are (essentially) the same numbers, so the output is the same \(\le 0\). The procedure never learns anything.

It's the same shape of argument as an adversary argument in complexity theory, or an information-theoretic lower bound. The escape route is not a better sieve; it is feeding the method extra information beyond the \(|\mathcal{A}_d|\). That's §6.

What the parity problem does not say
  • It does not say twin primes are infinite-or-not-provable. It says pure sieve methods cannot decide it.
  • It does not block upper bounds — those are fine, just off by a constant factor.
  • It does not block results about numbers with a bounded number of prime factors. Chen's theorem (every large even \(N\) is \(p + P_2\)) lives entirely inside the barrier, because \(P_2\) is parity-ambiguous by design.
  • It does not apply once you inject non-sieve input — bilinear (Type II) information, or the equidistribution that Zhang and Maynard exploit.

§ 5Dimension, and how far a sieve reaches

Sieves are classified by their dimension (or sifting density) \(\kappa\), defined by

\[ \prod_{w \le p < z}(1-g(p))^{-1} \sim \left(\frac{\log z}{\log w}\right)^{\kappa}. \]

Sifting the integers by all primes gives \(\kappa = 1\) (the linear sieve). Sifting \(n(n+2)\) removes two residue classes mod \(p\), so \(g(p) = 2/p\) and \(\kappa = 2\). Dimension is destiny: everything about a sieve's strength is a function of \(\kappa\).

Dimension = how many residue classes each prime deletes

"Cross out the multiples of \(p\)" is really "delete one residue class modulo \(p\)" — one of the \(p\) possible remainders. That removes a fraction \(1/p\) of everything, and the sieve has dimension \(\kappa = 1\).

Now try to sieve for twin primes. You want both \(n\) and \(n+2\) to survive, so you look at the product \(n(n+2)\). For a given prime \(p\), this is divisible by \(p\) when \(n \equiv 0\) or \(n \equiv -2\) modulo \(p\) — two classes instead of one. So \(g(p) = 2/p\), and \(\kappa = 2\).

Two different filters over the same stream. A dimension-1 sieve takes out every second car; a dimension-2 sieve takes out every second and every third. Far less gets through, so you know far less about what did — and the sieve's bounds degrade accordingly.
Why dimension is the only parameter that matters

Every quantitative feature of a sieve turns out to be a function of \(\kappa\) alone: how strong the upper bound is, how far off it is, and — crucially — at what point a lower bound becomes non-trivial. The factor-of-2 slack in dimension 1 becomes a factor of 4 in dimension 2.

Where the parity problem shows up in the formulas

The lemma below gives sandwich bounds with two factors \(F_\kappa\) (above) and \(f_\kappa\) (below). Both approach 1 if you sift gently enough. But look at what \(f_\kappa\) does when you sift hard: it goes to zero, and then below it. A "lower bound" that is negative tells you nothing — the count was obviously non-negative already.

For \(\kappa = 1\), \(f(2) = 0\) exactly. With Bombieri–Vinogradov giving level \(D = x^{1/2}\), \(s = 2\) corresponds to \(z = x^{1/4}\), so survivors may have up to three prime factors. That is precisely why classical sieves deliver \(P_3\) and not primes — and it is the parity problem of §4 reappearing, this time as a number in a table rather than an argument.

Fundamental lemma of sieve theory

With level \(D\) and \(s = \log D/\log z\), there are functions \(F_\kappa \ge 1 \ge f_\kappa\) with \[ X V(z) f_\kappa(s)\,(1+o(1)) \;\le\; S(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},z) \;\le\; XV(z)F_\kappa(s)\,(1+o(1)), \qquad V(z)=\prod_{p<z}(1-g(p)), \] and \(F_\kappa(s), f_\kappa(s) \to 1\) rapidly as \(s\to\infty\). Crucially, \(f_\kappa(s) \le 0\) for \(s\) below a threshold \(\beta_\kappa\) — and \(f_\kappa \le 0\) is exactly the parity problem showing up in the formulas.

§ 6Getting past the wall

6.1Bilinear forms

The parity obstruction assumes the method sees only \(|\mathcal{A}_d|\). Friedlander and Iwaniec's asymptotic sieve for primes and their theorem that \(x^2+y^4\) is prime infinitely often work because they supply an additional input: control of Type II (bilinear) sums \(\sum_{m}\sum_{n}\alpha_m\beta_n\,\mathbf{1}[mn \in \mathcal{A}]\). Such a sum is genuinely sensitive to how a number factors, which is the exact piece of information \(\lambda(n)\) hides. Once you have it, parity stops applying.

This is the same idea as Vinogradov's method for Goldbach's odd conjecture, and the same idea behind Vaughan's identity and the Type I / Type II decomposition. Modern analytic number theory is largely the study of how to obtain Type II information for sequences you care about.

6.2Bounded gaps — how it dodges parity

Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım, then Zhang (2013), then Maynard and Tao independently, proved \(\liminf_n (p_{n+1}-p_n) < \infty\). Note carefully what is proved: some bounded gap occurs infinitely often, not gap 2. That distinction is the parity problem being respected, not broken.

The mechanism: take an admissible tuple \(\{h_1,\dots,h_k\}\) and a Selberg-type weight \(w_n\). Show \(\sum_n w_n\left(\sum_i \mathbf{1}[n+h_i \text{ prime}] - m\right) > 0\). A positive sum forces some \(n\) with at least \(m+1\) primes among \(n+h_i\) — but tells you nothing about which \(i\). The sieve never has to distinguish a prime from a \(P_2\) at a specified location, so parity is sidestepped rather than defeated. Maynard's improvement was to use a multidimensional weight \(\lambda_{d_1,\dots,d_k}\) instead of a one-dimensional one, which is why his method also gives bounded gaps between \(m\) primes for every \(m\).

§ 7Check yourself

Legendre's sieve is an exact identity. Why is it useless for counting primes up to \(x\)?

Exactness is not the problem — the problem is that exactness costs one error term per squarefree divisor of \(P(z)\), and there are \(2^{\pi(z)}\) of them. The usable range for \(z\) grows like \(\log x\) while you need \(\sqrt x\). Fig. 2 shows the crossing point barely moving as \(x\) goes from \(10^3\) to \(10^{30}\).

In Fig. 5, setting \(D = 30\) and \(\lambda = \mu\) hits the optimum exactly. What does that demonstrate?

At full level, \(\left(\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\right)^2 = \mathbf{1}[n=1]\) — the square is an equality, not an inequality, so the bound is exact. Restricting to \(d\le D\) is what introduces slack. This reframes the whole subject: you are not limited by the sieve, you are limited by your level of distribution, which is why Bombieri–Vinogradov and Elliott–Halberstam are the load-bearing theorems.

A colleague claims a new sieve inequality that proves infinitely many twin primes. Before reading it, what do you know?

This is the practical value of knowing the parity problem: it is a filter you can apply before investing time. The only way through is extra input — bilinear/Type II information, or a formulation (like bounded gaps) that never requires distinguishing a prime from a \(P_2\) at a named location.

Why did Zhang's theorem give "gaps below 70 million" rather than "twin primes"?

The size of the bound is an artefact (Polymath8 later drove it to 246, and to 6 under generalised Elliott–Halberstam); the shape of the result is structural. "At least two of \(n+h_1,\dots,n+h_k\) are prime" is a statement a parity-respecting method can prove; "\(n\) and \(n+2\) are both prime" is not. Even assuming every conjecture in sight, the method reaches gap 6, not 2.
