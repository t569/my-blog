Mathematics as a string of symbols

Before Gödel's theorems can say anything, mathematical language has to become a mathematical object: an alphabet, a grammar, a mechanical notion of proof. Once it is an object, it can be counted — and once it can be counted, it can talk about itself.

§ 0The one-sentence version

Formal proof is a mechanical relation between finite strings; finite strings can be coded as numbers; therefore a theory that can reason about numbers can reason about its own proofs — and once it can, self-reference is unavoidable and a gap opens between true and provable.

Notice how much of the work happens before any theorem. Three quarters of Gödel's 1931 paper is bookkeeping: defining the language, defining proof, defining the encoding, and grinding through forty-six primitive recursive functions. The famous part is four pages. This volume spends its first third on the bookkeeping, because that is where the real ideas are and where most expositions skip.

Why does mathematics need a formal language at all? Ordinary mathematical prose is fine for communicating between humans, but it is hopeless as an object of study. You cannot prove a theorem about "all possible proofs" if a proof is a persuasive essay.

So: fix a finite alphabet. Fix a grammar saying which strings count as statements. Fix a finite list of axioms and a finite list of rules, each of which is a purely typographical operation on strings. Now "proof" means "a finite list of strings where each one is an axiom or follows from earlier ones by a rule" — something a machine can check without understanding anything.

Once proof is that concrete, it becomes something you can do mathematics to. That move — Hilbert's move — is what makes Gödel's theorems possible. It also makes Lean possible (Vol. IV).

Two things that are constantly confused

Gödel's completeness theorem (1929, his thesis) says first-order logic is complete: anything true in every model is provable. Gödel's incompleteness theorem (1931) says a specific theory (PA, ZFC, …) fails to prove some sentence true in its intended model. These are not in tension. The first is about logic; the second is about theories. Keep them apart or nothing in this subject will make sense.

§ 1How the language is built

We construct the language of arithmetic from nothing. Everything is a finite string over a finite alphabet; nothing means anything until §1.4.

1.1Alphabet

Fix these symbols and no others:

constants   0
functions   S (unary)   +  ·  (binary)
relations   =   <
logical     ¬   ∧   ∨   →   ∀   ∃
grouping    (   )
variables   v₀  v₁  v₂  …

That is the entire vocabulary of arithmetic. There is no symbol for "prime", "divides", "continuous", or "set" — everything else must be defined as an abbreviation for a string built from these.

That really is the whole vocabulary

Look at what is missing from that list. No symbol for "prime". None for "divides", "greater than or equal", "is a set", "continuous", "group". Not even subtraction or division.

This is deliberate, and it is the point. Every other notion must be defined — that is, written out as an abbreviation for a string built from these few symbols. "\(x\) divides \(y\)" becomes E z. (y = (x * z)). "\(x\) is prime" becomes a longer string saying "\(x > 1\) and whenever \(x\) is a product, one factor is 1". The abbreviations are for our convenience; the formal system never sees them.

A computer's processor has a small instruction set — add, compare, jump, load, store. Everything you have ever run was built from those. Nobody writes programs that way, but the fact that it is possible is what lets you reason about what a computer can and cannot do. Same move here, for the same reason.
Why a small alphabet is worth the inconvenience

Because we are about to prove a theorem about every possible proof. You cannot do that if a proof is a persuasive essay, and you cannot do it easily if the language has a thousand primitives. Each symbol is another case in every induction. Gödel needed to verify by hand that forty-six different operations on these strings were mechanically computable — with a richer alphabet, that would have been hopeless.

So: the language is deliberately miserable to write in, and correspondingly easy to reason about. That trade is the whole of Hilbert's programme, and it is also exactly why Lean's kernel can be a few thousand lines (Vol. IV).

1.2Terms and formulas, defined inductively

Definition

Terms are the smallest set such that: 0 is a term; every variable is a term; and if \(s,t\) are terms then so are S(t), (s + t), (s · t).

Atomic formulas are strings s = t and s < t for terms \(s,t\).

Formulas are the smallest set containing the atomic formulas and closed under: if \(\varphi,\psi\) are formulas and \(v\) a variable, then ¬φ, (φ ∧ ψ), (φ ∨ ψ), (φ → ψ), ∀v φ, ∃v φ are formulas.

"The smallest set closed under …" is doing real work: it licenses proof by induction on the structure of a formula, which is the workhorse of every proof in logic and the reason Lean's inductive types (Vol. IV, §3) look the way they do.

A variable occurrence is bound if it lies inside a \(\forall v\) or \(\exists v\) for that same \(v\), and free otherwise. A formula with no free variables is a sentence; only sentences have truth values.

Build some and see. The parser below is a real recursive-descent parser over the grammar above — its error messages are the authentic experience of formal syntax.

"The smallest set closed under…" — what that phrase is doing

The definitions above have an unusual shape. Rather than saying what a formula is, they say how to build one, and then add: nothing else counts.

Read it as a two-part recipe. Base cases: the atomic formulas are formulas. Building steps: if \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) are formulas, so are \(\neg\varphi\), \((\varphi \wedge \psi)\), \(\forall v\,\varphi\), and the rest. Closure clause: and nothing is a formula unless it was forced to be by finitely many applications of those rules.

It is exactly how you would define "reachable station" on a rail network. Base case: you are at station A. Step: if you can reach X and there is a line from X to Y, you can reach Y. Closure: nothing else is reachable. That last clause is what lets you prove a station is unreachable — without it, you could only ever demonstrate that things are reachable.
The closure clause is what licenses induction

Because every formula is built in finitely many steps from atoms, you can prove a property \(P\) holds of all formulas by doing two things: check \(P\) for the atoms, and check that each building step preserves \(P\). That is structural induction, and it is the workhorse of every proof in this volume.

It is the same argument as ordinary induction on \(\mathbb{N}\) — which is itself defined the same way: zero is a number, the successor of a number is a number, nothing else is. The dominoes fall in a tree instead of a line, but they fall for the same reason.

Hold on to this. In Vol. IV you will meet Lean's inductive types, which are this definition style promoted to a programming language feature, and Lean's recursors, which are structural induction turned into a piece of data you can apply. They are not an analogy for what is happening here. They are the same thing.

1.3Proof, as a typographical relation

A deductive system is a decidable set of axioms plus finitely many rules. The classic Hilbert-style presentation for propositional logic needs three axiom schemes and one rule:

A1.  φ → (ψ → φ)
A2.  (φ → (ψ → χ)) → ((φ → ψ) → (φ → χ))
A3.  (¬ψ → ¬φ) → (φ → ψ)
MP.  from φ and φ → ψ, infer ψ

A proof of \(\varphi\) is a finite sequence of formulas ending in \(\varphi\), each of which is an axiom instance or follows from two earlier ones by MP. Checking a proof requires no understanding whatever — it is pattern-matching on strings. That is exactly what makes it arithmetizable in §2, and exactly what makes Lean's kernel possible in Vol. IV.

To feel how mechanical it is, derive \(p \to p\). It takes five lines and no insight — only bookkeeping.

1.4Meaning arrives last

Only now do we say what any of it means. An \(L\)-structure \(\mathcal{M}\) is a set \(M\) with an element for 0, functions for S, +, ·, and relations for =, <. Tarski's recursive definition of satisfaction \(\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[\bar a]\) then assigns truth values by induction on formula structure.

Two relations now exist and must be kept apart:

  • \(T \vdash \varphi\) — syntactic. There exists a finite string that is a proof. Semi-decidable: search all strings.
  • \(T \models \varphi\) — semantic. \(\varphi\) holds in every model of \(T\). Quantifies over a proper class of structures.
Gödel's completeness theorem (1929)

For first-order logic, \(T \vdash \varphi \iff T \models \varphi\).

This is a genuine miracle and it is the reason incompleteness has the shape it does. Because \(\vdash\) and \(\models\) coincide, "PA does not prove \(\varphi\)" is equivalent to "there is a model of PA where \(\varphi\) fails". Independence results are therefore always model constructions in disguise — see §6.

Why isn't completeness already the whole story? Completeness says: if \(\varphi\) is true in all models of \(T\), then \(T\) proves it. Incompleteness produces a \(\varphi\) that is true in the standard model \(\mathbb{N}\) but false in some other model of PA. So \(\varphi\) is not true in all models of PA, and completeness is not violated — it is confirmed.

The uncomfortable content is: PA has models other than \(\mathbb{N}\), and no first-order theory can pin \(\mathbb{N}\) down. This is not Gödel's theorem, it's a consequence of compactness, and it's arguably the deeper fact.

§ 2Arithmetization: strings become numbers

Gödel's device: assign each symbol a code, then encode a string \(s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n\) as

\[ \ulcorner s \urcorner = 2^{c(s_1)}\cdot 3^{c(s_2)}\cdot 5^{c(s_3)}\cdots p_n^{c(s_n)}. \]

Unique factorisation makes this injective and invertible, and — crucially — every operation on strings (concatenation, substitution, "is the \(i\)-th symbol a left bracket") becomes a primitive recursive function of codes.

The coding itself is not clever. That is the point.

Assign each symbol a number. Then encode the string \(s_1s_2\cdots s_n\) as \(2^{c(s_1)}\cdot3^{c(s_2)}\cdot5^{c(s_3)}\cdots\), using the primes in order as bases and the symbol codes as exponents.

Why is this reversible? Because of unique factorisation: every positive integer factors into primes in exactly one way. So from the number you can recover the exponent of 2, of 3, of 5 — and those are your symbols back, in order. Nothing is lost.

It is a filing system, no more. Compare storing a word as a single number in base 27: CAT becomes \(3\cdot27^2 + 1\cdot27 + 20\). You can always decode, because base-27 representation is unique. Gödel used primes rather than a base because in 1931 he had to verify by hand that extracting the \(i\)th symbol was a mechanically computable operation, and "how many times does 5 divide this number" is very easy to write down that way.
The numbers are astronomical, and it does not matter at all

Encoding a short formula produces an integer with thousands of digits — more than the atoms in the observable universe, by a wide margin. This bothers people, and it should not. Nobody ever computes these numbers. The argument requires only that they exist and that the operations on them be definable. Try the base-32 packing toggle in Fig. 3: it produces vastly smaller numbers and changes nothing, because nothing ever depended on the choice.

What the coding does not yet give you

Here is the step people skip. Turning proofs into numbers is worthless by itself. What you need is for the theory — which only talks about numbers — to be able to say things about the coded relations. "There exists a \(p\) such that \(p\) codes a proof of the formula coded by \(f\)" has to be expressible as an honest formula of arithmetic, built from \(0, S, +, \cdot\) and quantifiers.

That is what §2.1 supplies, and it is the genuinely hard bookkeeping of Gödel's paper. Once you have it, the theory can express "I can prove this" — and from there the rest is four pages.

2.1Representability — the bridge

Coding is useless unless the theory can talk about the coded relations. That is the content of:

Representability theorem

Every recursive function \(f\) is representable in Robinson arithmetic \(Q\): there is a formula \(\Phi_f(x,y)\) with \(Q \vdash \forall y\,(\Phi_f(\bar n, y) \leftrightarrow y = \overline{f(n)})\). Every recursive relation is similarly definable, and every \(\Sigma_1\) truth is provable in \(Q\) (\(\Sigma_1\)-completeness).

Apply it to the relation \(\mathrm{Prf}_T(p, f)\): "\(p\) codes a \(T\)-proof of the formula coded by \(f\)". Because proof-checking is mechanical (§1.3), this relation is primitive recursive, so there's a \(\Delta_0\) formula defining it. Then

\[ \mathrm{Prov}_T(f) \;:=\; \exists p\, \mathrm{Prf}_T(p,f) \]

is \(\Sigma_1\). The theory can now express "I can prove this". Note the asymmetry that runs through everything downstream: \(\mathrm{Prov}\) has an unbounded existential, so provability is semi-decidable, not decidable — you can confirm a proof exists by finding it, but never confirm none exists by searching.

Where the hypotheses live

"\(T\) is a theory" is not enough — the axiom set must be decidable (or at least computably enumerable), otherwise \(\mathrm{Prf}_T\) is not recursive and nothing above works. This is why "the set of all true arithmetic sentences" evades incompleteness: it is complete and consistent, but not computably axiomatisable, so you cannot write down its \(\mathrm{Prov}\). Tarski's undefinability theorem is the matching statement: truth is not definable in the language, even though provability is.

§ 3Self-reference, made safe

The liar paradox — "this sentence is false" — is genuinely paradoxical. Gödel's sentence is not, and the difference is entirely in §2: provability is definable, truth is not. Replacing "false" with "unprovable" converts a paradox into a theorem.

The objection you should be raising

"You are defining a sentence in terms of itself. That is circular. I could define anything that way."

It is a good objection, and answering it properly is the entire content of §3. The answer is: nothing is defined in terms of itself. The sentence \(G\) is constructed by a finite, mechanical procedure that at no point mentions \(G\). It only turns out afterwards — as a discovered fact, not a stipulation — that the sentence so constructed happens to be about its own code.

A program cannot contain a literal copy of its own source text; that would have to be infinitely long. But it can contain a short recipe which, when run on itself, reproduces the text. That is a quine, and you can run one in Fig. 4. No circularity — just a description applied to itself.
The three ingredients

One: substitution is a mechanical string operation. "Take formula number \(n\), and put the numeral \(m\) in place of its free variable." Tedious, but obviously computable — so by §2.1 it is representable by a formula.

Two: the diagonal move. Build \(\beta(x)\) saying "whatever you get by substituting \(x\) into formula number \(x\), \(\psi\) holds of it". Note the doubled \(x\) — a formula is being fed its own number. This is the only clever step in the whole construction.

Three: take \(\beta\)'s own code \(b\) and form \(\theta = \beta(\bar b)\). This is an ordinary sentence, produced by ordinary substitution. And now the fact falls out: \(\theta\) is the result of substituting \(b\) into formula \(b\), so its own code is exactly what \(\beta\) was talking about. \(\theta\) says \(\psi(\ulcorner\theta\urcorner)\).

The crucial generality

Notice that \(\psi\) was never specified. The construction works for any property. Feed it "is not provable" and you get Gödel's sentence. Feed it "is provable" and you get Henkin's sentence, which by Löb's theorem turns out to be provable. Feed it "is false" and you get the liar — which is why Tarski's theorem says truth cannot be a formula in the first place.

The diagonal lemma is a theorem about substitution, not about paradox. Learn it that way and Gödel, Tarski, Löb, Rice's theorem and Kleene's recursion theorem become one lemma wearing five hats.

The mechanism is a fixed-point construction that has nothing to do with logic per se. You have met it before, in programming.

§ 4The first incompleteness theorem

Gödel–Rosser

Let \(T\) be a consistent, computably axiomatised theory interpreting Robinson arithmetic \(Q\). Then there is a sentence \(\varphi\) with \(T \nvdash \varphi\) and \(T \nvdash \neg\varphi\).

The argument, given §2 and §3, is four lines:

  1. Get \(G\) with \(T \vdash G \leftrightarrow \neg\mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner G\urcorner)\).
  2. If \(T \vdash G\), take a proof; its code \(p\) satisfies \(\mathrm{Prf}_T(p, \ulcorner G\urcorner)\), which is a true \(\Delta_0\) fact, so by \(\Sigma_1\)-completeness \(T \vdash \mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner G \urcorner)\), i.e. \(T\vdash\neg G\). So \(T\) is inconsistent. Contradiction.
  3. Hence \(T \nvdash G\). So \(\mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner G\urcorner)\) is false, so \(G\) is true (in \(\mathbb{N}\)).
  4. \(T\nvdash \neg G\) needs a little more care — Gödel assumed \(\omega\)-consistency; Rosser's refinement replaces \(\mathrm{Prov}\) with "there is a proof of \(\varphi\) with no shorter proof of \(\neg\varphi\)", which needs only consistency.

Step 3 is where "true but unprovable" comes from, and it is worth staring at: the truth of \(G\) is established by us, reasoning in the metatheory about \(\mathbb{N}\). It is not an extra axiom, not mysticism, and not evidence that minds exceed machines — it is an ordinary argument that uses assumptions (the consistency of \(T\)) that \(T\) itself cannot use about itself.

4.1What it does not say

This theorem is the most misquoted result in mathematics. Open each claim only after deciding whether it's true.

"Gödel proved that some mathematical truths can never be proved."

Nearly right and importantly wrong. \(G\) is unprovable in \(T\). It is perfectly provable in \(T + \mathrm{Con}(T)\), or in ZFC if \(T\) is PA. There is no sentence that is unprovable in every reasonable system — indeed \(G\) has a short proof once you are allowed to assume \(T\) is consistent, which every working mathematician does.

The theorem is about the relationship between a theory and its own consistency, not about an absolute barrier to knowledge.

"Therefore mathematics is inconsistent / unreliable / built on sand."

It says the opposite. Incompleteness is a consequence of consistency: an inconsistent theory proves everything and is perfectly complete. The theorem is "if you are consistent, you are incomplete" — being incomplete is the good case.

"Human minds can see G is true, so minds surpass computers."

The Lucas–Penrose argument. The standard objection: what we can actually see is the conditional "if \(T\) is consistent then \(G\) is true" — and that conditional is provable inside \(T\) itself (it is essentially the second incompleteness theorem). Establishing the antecedent for one's own reasoning is exactly what no system, human or machine, gets for free.

A machine given the axiom \(\mathrm{Con}(T)\) sees \(G\) as easily as we do. The asymmetry is about which system we are standing in, not about silicon.

"Every sufficiently strong theory is incomplete, so all foundations are equally arbitrary."

Incompleteness is uniform, but theories are not interchangeable. They form a rich, strictly increasing hierarchy of consistency strength — PA ⊂ ATR₀ ⊂ Z₂ ⊂ ZFC ⊂ ZFC + large cardinals — and where a theorem sits in that hierarchy is real mathematical information (this is the programme of reverse mathematics). "All incomplete" no more makes theories equivalent than "all finite" makes groups equivalent.

"Gödel's sentence is a contrived logician's trick with no bearing on real mathematics."

This was defensible until 1977. Then Paris–Harrington gave a natural finite Ramsey-theoretic statement independent of PA, and Kirby–Paris gave Goodstein's theorem and the hydra game. All are statements a combinatorialist might have asked about for their own sake. See §7 — you can run one.

For ZFC, the continuum hypothesis and a long list of set-theoretic statements are independent, and independence results now routinely appear in group theory, topology and analysis.

"It means there are things we will never know."

Epistemologically overloaded. What the theorem delivers is a precise relation: no c.e. consistent theory extending \(Q\) proves its own consistency. Anything beyond that — about human knowledge, physics, or the mind — is philosophy with Gödel's name attached, and usually smuggles in premises the theorem does not supply.

§ 5The second theorem, and Löb

Formalise the proof of step 2 above inside \(T\). It shows \(T \vdash \mathrm{Con}(T) \to G\). Since \(T\nvdash G\), we get \(T \nvdash \mathrm{Con}(T)\).

Second incompleteness theorem

If \(T\) is consistent, computably axiomatised, and interprets enough arithmetic, then \(T \nvdash \mathrm{Con}(T)\), where \(\mathrm{Con}(T) :\equiv \neg\mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)\).

"Enough arithmetic" here is more than \(Q\): the formalisation needs the Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions, writing \(\Box\varphi\) for \(\mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner)\):

\[ \text{D1: } T\vdash\varphi \Rightarrow T \vdash \Box\varphi, \qquad \text{D2: } T\vdash \Box(\varphi\to\psi)\to(\Box\varphi\to\Box\psi), \qquad \text{D3: } T \vdash \Box\varphi \to \Box\Box\varphi. \]

D3 is the demanding one — it requires \(T\) to prove \(\Sigma_1\)-completeness about itself, which needs induction, which is why \(Q\) alone is not enough for the second theorem though it suffices for the first.

Why is "I can't prove my own consistency" so much worse than it sounds — or so much better? Both, depending on where you stand.

Worse: Hilbert's programme wanted to justify infinitary mathematics by a finitary consistency proof. The second theorem says the finitary part (formalisable in PA) cannot even certify itself, let alone the rest. The programme in its original form is dead.

Better: consistency proofs still exist, they just have to use genuinely stronger principles. Gentzen (1936) proved Con(PA) using transfinite induction up to \(\varepsilon_0\) — a principle that is not provable in PA but is intuitively far more evident than the full strength of PA. That trade turned out to be enormously productive; it is the origin of ordinal analysis and of §7.

Löb's theorem (1955)

If \(T \vdash \Box\varphi \to \varphi\), then \(T \vdash \varphi\).

Startling on first reading: you cannot safely assert "if this is provable then it's true" about any particular sentence unless it is already provable. Taking \(\varphi \equiv 0=1\) recovers the second theorem, since \(\Box(0{=}1)\to 0{=}1\) is exactly \(\mathrm{Con}(T)\) in contrapositive. The proof is the diagonal lemma again, applied to \(\psi(x) :\equiv \mathrm{Prov}_T(x) \to \varphi\).

Löb's theorem is what makes provability logic work: the modal logic GL (axiom \(\Box(\Box\varphi\to\varphi)\to\Box\varphi\)) is, by Solovay's completeness theorem, exactly the set of schemes about provability that PA proves. A decidable modal logic completely characterises PA's view of itself — one of the most satisfying results in the subject.

§ 6What a model of \(\mathrm{PA} + \neg\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{PA})\) looks like

By completeness (§1.4), \(\mathrm{PA}\nvdash\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{PA})\) means there is a model \(\mathcal{M}\models \mathrm{PA}+\neg\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{PA})\). In \(\mathcal{M}\) there is an element \(c\) that \(\mathcal{M}\) believes codes a proof of \(0=1\). Since PA really is consistent, \(c\) cannot be a standard natural number. So \(\mathcal{M}\) contains elements beyond \(\mathbb{N}\).

Every countable nonstandard model of PA has the same order type: \(\mathbb{N}\) followed by a dense order of copies of \(\mathbb{Z}\), with no first and no last copy — order type \(\mathbb{N} + \mathbb{Z}\cdot\mathbb{Q}\).

How there can be extra natural numbers

This is the part that sounds like a trick and is not. The claim is that there are structures satisfying every single axiom of PA — including every instance of induction — which nonetheless contain elements beyond \(0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\).

Here is the construction, and it needs nothing from Gödel. Add a brand-new constant symbol \(c\) to the language, together with infinitely many new axioms: \(c > 0\), \(c > 1\), \(c > 2\), and so on forever.

Now ask whether this is contradictory. Take any finite collection of these axioms — say the ones up to \(c > 1000\). Is it satisfiable? Obviously: work in ordinary \(\mathbb{N}\) and let \(c = 1001\). Every finite piece is fine.

And the compactness theorem says that if every finite piece of a set of axioms has a model, the whole set does. (Why? Because proofs are finite. If the infinite set were contradictory, some finite subset would already be.) So there is a structure where \(c\) exceeds every ordinary number.

Compactness is a promise about finiteness, not about infinity. A contradiction, if there is one, has to show up in a finite argument — so if no finite part is broken, nothing is.
Why the model cannot notice

Surely \(\mathcal{M}\) could just define "the standard numbers" as those reachable from 0 by finitely many successor steps, and observe that \(c\) is not among them?

No — and the reason is precise. Induction in PA is a scheme: one axiom for each formula. It applies to definable properties only, not to arbitrary collections. And "is a standard natural number" is not definable in the language. So \(\mathcal{M}\) has no way to formulate the distinction, and induction never gets a chance to rule the extra elements out.

What this does to "true but unprovable"

Everything becomes concrete. A model of PA + ¬Con(PA) contains an element \(c\) that it believes codes a proof of \(0=1\). Since PA really is consistent, \(c\) is nonstandard. The model can check, step by internal step, that \(c\) is a valid proof — but the checking takes nonstandardly many steps, so no contradiction ever reaches us.

"True but unprovable" always means: true in \(\mathbb{N}\), false in some other model. Incompleteness is not a hole in mathematics. It is the shadow cast by the fact that first-order axioms cannot single out \(\mathbb{N}\) — and compactness guarantees that before Gödel says a word.

The moral

"True but unprovable" always means: true in \(\mathbb{N}\), false in some other model. Incompleteness is not a hole in mathematics; it is the shadow cast by the fact that first-order axioms cannot single out \(\mathbb{N}\) among all their models. Compactness guarantees the extra models exist before Gödel says a word.

§ 7An independent statement you can actually run

Goodstein's theorem is a genuine combinatorial statement, independent of PA (Kirby–Paris, 1982). Here is the process:

  1. Write \(n\) in hereditary base 2: as a sum of powers of 2, where the exponents are themselves written in hereditary base 2, recursively. So \(266 = 2^{2^3}+2^3+2 = 2^{2^{2+1}}+2^{2+1}+2\).
  2. Bump the base: replace every 2 by 3. Then subtract 1.
  3. Repeat, bumping to 4, 5, 6, …
Goodstein, 1944

Every Goodstein sequence reaches 0.

The values grow so violently that this looks obviously false. The proof is three lines: replace every base by \(\omega\) to get an ordinal below \(\varepsilon_0\); base-bumping does not change the ordinal; subtracting 1 strictly decreases it; \(\varepsilon_0\) is well-ordered, so no infinite descent. Watch both columns at once.

Do one by hand, with \(n = 3\)

Step 1. Write 3 in hereditary base 2: \(3 = 2 + 1\). (Hereditary means the exponents are themselves written this way — here there are none to worry about.)

Step 2. Bump base 2 to base 3, giving \(3 + 1 = 4\). Then subtract 1: 3.

Step 3. Write 3 in hereditary base 3: just \(3\). Bump to base 4: \(4\). Subtract 1: 3.

Then 3 in base 4 is \(3\); bumping does nothing to a bare digit; subtract 1 gives 2. Then 1. Then 0. Five steps and it is done.

Now try \(n=4\) in the figure. \(4 = 2^2\). Bump: \(3^3 = 27\). Subtract 1: 26. The values rocket past \(10^{100}\) within a dozen steps and the sequence does not terminate until step \(3\cdot2^{402653211}-1\). It looks obviously divergent.

Why it terminates anyway

Watch the second column instead. Take the hereditary base-\(b\) form and replace every \(b\) by the symbol \(\omega\). You get an ordinal — a transfinite counting number.

Now the two operations, one at a time. Base-bumping changes every \(b\) to \(b+1\) — but since you replaced all of them by \(\omega\) anyway, the ordinal is completely unchanged. It is invisible to the substitution. Subtracting 1 genuinely lowers the ordinal.

So the ordinal never increases, and strictly decreases at every step. And ordinals below \(\varepsilon_0\) are well-ordered: there is no infinite strictly decreasing sequence, any more than there is one in \(0,1,2,3,\dots\). A sequence that strictly decreases must therefore stop.

You are descending an infinitely tall staircase whose steps have no smallest size — but every move takes you down at least one step, and the staircase has a bottom. However astronomically long it takes, you arrive. The integers exploding is a distraction; the ordinal is the quantity that is actually in charge.
And why PA cannot run that argument

The proof needs one thing PA does not have: transfinite induction up to \(\varepsilon_0\). Gentzen showed that is exactly what PA is missing — adding it proves Con(PA), which by the second theorem PA cannot do alone.

So Goodstein's theorem is a perfectly ordinary statement about integers, provable by three lines of ordinal reasoning, and independent of Peano arithmetic. It was the answer to the standing objection that Gödel sentences are contrived logicians' tricks — this is a statement a combinatorialist might have asked about for its own sake.

§ 8Check yourself

Gödel proved both a completeness theorem and an incompleteness theorem. Are they in tension?

\(G\) is true in \(\mathbb{N}\) and false in some other model of PA, so it is not a semantic consequence of PA — completeness is confirmed, not violated. The two theorems together give the sharpest formulation: unprovable and has a countermodel are the same thing, so every independence result is a model construction.

"The set of all true sentences of arithmetic" is consistent and complete. Why doesn't it refute Gödel?

Decidability of the axiom set is a genuine hypothesis, not fine print. Tarski's theorem makes it precise: arithmetic truth is not even definable in the language of arithmetic, let alone recursively axiomatisable. Provability is definable (it's \(\Sigma_1\)); truth is not. That single asymmetry is what turns the liar paradox into Gödel's theorem.

The diagonal lemma builds \(\theta\) with \(T \vdash \theta \leftrightarrow \psi(\ulcorner\theta\urcorner)\). What is it fundamentally a theorem about?

The lemma mentions neither truth nor provability; \(\psi\) is arbitrary. That generality is why the same construction yields Gödel, Tarski, Löb, Rice's theorem, and Kleene's recursion theorem. Recognising a diagonal argument as "a fixed point of a computable transformation" rather than "a paradox" is the single most portable idea in this volume.

In Fig. 6, the Goodstein value for \(n=4\) rockets past \(10^{100}\) while the ordinal drops from \(\omega^\omega\) downward. Why does the ordinal settle the question?

Base-bumping leaves the ordinal unchanged (that's the point — it is invisible to the \(\omega\)-substitution); only the "subtract 1" moves it, and always downward. Well-ordering then finishes the argument in one line. PA cannot run this argument because it cannot prove transfinite induction up to \(\varepsilon_0\) — and Gentzen showed that principle is exactly what PA is missing, since adding it proves Con(PA).
